## LO1 — The Activities and Users of Accounting

### What is Accounting? Three basic activities

> *"Accounting consists of three basic activities. It **identifies, records, and communicates** an organization's economic events to interested users."*

![[three_activities.png]]

Let's define each activity precisely, exactly as the slide states:

1. **Identification** — deciding *which* economic events even count as something accounting should track. The slide's examples: **Sales, Provision of Services, Payment of Wages.** Not every single thing that happens in a business is an "economic event" worth recording (e.g., hiring a new employee isn't itself a transaction, but paying that employee's first wage is) — identification is the filtering step where you decide what's actually a business transaction.

2. **Recording** — described as *"a systematic, chronological diary of events."* Two key words here: **systematic** (following a consistent, structured method — not just jotting notes randomly) and **chronological** (in the order they happened, date by date). This is literally what bookkeeping is.

3. **Communication** — getting the recorded information out to the people who need it, via **accounting reports (financial statements)**. This activity itself breaks into two further pieces:
   - **Analysis and interpretation** — using ratios, percentages, graphs, and charts to highlight significant financial trends and relationships (turning raw numbers into insight).
   - **Interpretation** specifically involves *explaining the uses, meaning, and limitations of reported data* — i.e., not just showing a number, but explaining what it means and where it might mislead someone if misread.

**Illustration 1-1 (above)** draws this as a left-to-right pipeline: a person selecting economic events (Identification) → someone at a computer who **records, classifies, and summarizes** (Recording) → a person preparing accounting reports and others analyzing/interpreting them for users (Communication).

> 📌 **Important boxed note directly from the slide:** *"The accounting process includes the **bookkeeping** function."* This is a commonly tested distinction: **bookkeeping is only part of accounting** — specifically, it's mostly the "Recording" activity. Accounting is the bigger picture that also includes identification and communication/analysis.

### Who Uses Accounting Data — Internal Users

![[internal_users.png]]

**Internal users** are people *inside* the organization who use accounting information to run the business day-to-day. The slide gives one example question per department:

| Internal User | Example question they'd ask |
|---|---|
| **Marketing** | What price should Apple charge for an iPod to maximize the company's net income? |
| **Management** | Which PepsiCo product line is the most profitable? Should any product lines be eliminated? |
| **Finance** | Is cash sufficient to pay dividends to Microsoft stockholders? |
| **Human Resources** | Can General Motors afford to give its employees pay raises this year? |

The pattern to notice: every internal-user question is about **running the business better** — pricing, product decisions, cash management, staffing costs. These are operational, day-to-day decisions.

### Who Uses Accounting Data — External Users

![[external_users_1.png]]

**External users** are people *outside* the organization who don't run the business, but still need to know its financial condition to make their own decisions:

| External User | Example question they'd ask |
|---|---|
| **Creditors** | Will United Airlines be able to pay its debts as they come due? |
| **Investors** | How does Disney compare in size and profitability with Time Warner? |
| **Investors** | Is General Electric earning satisfactory income? |

**Other External Users** listed explicitly on the slide (memorize this list — it's a common short-answer question):
1. Auditors
2. Taxing Authorities (in Bangladesh: **NBR** — National Board of Revenue)
3. Regulatory Agencies (in Bangladesh: **BSEC** — Bangladesh Securities and Exchange Commission)
4. Customers
5. Labor Unions

**The core distinction, in one line:** internal users make decisions *for* the company (operating decisions); external users make decisions *about* the company (whether to lend to it, invest in it, tax it, regulate it, or trust it as a customer/employee).

### Branches of Accounting

> *"There are two major branches of accounting. They are **Financial Accounting** & **Managerial Accounting**."*

- **Financial Accounting:** *"involves recording and classifying business transactions, and preparing and presenting financial statements to be used by internal and external users."* — notice it serves **both** internal and external users.
- **Managerial Accounting:** *"focuses on providing information for use by internal users, the management."* — this one serves **only** internal users.

The slide also notes: *"There are other types of accounting as well such as Cost Accounting, Forensic Accounting, Auditing, Tax Accounting etc."*

## LO2 — Ethics, Principles, and Assumptions (the "building blocks")

### Ethics in Financial Reporting

- Recent financial scandals cited: **Enron, WorldCom, HealthSouth, AIG**, and other companies.
- *"Regulators and lawmakers are concerned that the economy would suffer if investors lost confidence in corporate accounting."* This is *why* ethics gets its own section in an accounting course — the whole system of investing and lending depends on people trusting that financial numbers are honestly reported.
- *"Effective financial reporting depends on sound ethical behavior."* — the one-line takeaway.

### IFAC Code of Ethics (International Federation of Accountants)

Five principles, each defined precisely:

- **Integrity** — being *"straightforward and honest in all professional and business relationships."*
- **Objectivity** — accountants *"should not allow bias, conflict of interest, or undue influence."*
- **Professional Competence and Due Care** — *"a continuing duty to maintain professional knowledge and skill."* (Note: "continuing" — this isn't a one-time qualification, it means staying updated throughout your career.)
- **Confidentiality** — *"should respect the confidentiality of information acquired"* and *"should not disclose any such information to third parties unless there is a legal or professional right or duty to disclose."* (Notice the built-in exception: confidentiality isn't absolute — if the law requires disclosure, or there's a professional duty to disclose, it's allowed.)
- **Professional Behavior** — *"should comply with relevant laws and regulations"* and *"should avoid any action that discredits the profession."*

### Ethical Threats (5 types — commonly asked to list + define)

- **Self-interest threats** — *"may occur as a result of the financial or other interests of a professional accountant or of an immediate or close family member."* (e.g., an accountant auditing a company they personally own shares in.)
- **Self-review threats** — *"may occur when a previous judgment needs to be re-evaluated by the professional accountant."* (e.g., auditing your own earlier work — hard to be objective about your own mistakes.)
- **Advocacy threats** — *"may occur when a professional accountant promotes a position or opinion to the point that subsequent objectivity may be compromised."* (e.g., publicly arguing so strongly for a client's position that you can no longer judge it fairly.)
- **Familiarity threats** — *"may occur when, because of a close relationship, a professional accountant becomes too sympathetic to the interests of others."* (e.g., auditing a close friend's business.)
- **Intimidation threats** — *"may occur when a professional accountant may be deterred from acting objectively by threats, actual or perceived."* (e.g., a client threatening to fire the auditor if they don't get the report they want.)

### Steps in Analyzing Ethical Situations

![[steps_analyzing_ethics.png]]

Three sequential steps, exactly as worded on the slide:

1. **"Recognize an ethical situation and the ethical issues involved."** — *"Use your personal ethics to identify ethical situations and issues. Some businesses and professional organizations provide written codes of ethics for guidance in some business situations."*
2. **"Identify and analyze the principal elements in the situation."** — *"Identify the **stakeholders** — persons or groups who may be harmed or benefited. Ask the question: What are the responsibilities and obligations of the parties involved?"*
3. **"Identify the alternatives, and weigh the impact of each alternative on various stakeholders."** — *"Select the most ethical alternative, considering all the consequences. Sometimes there will be one right answer. Other situations involve more than one right solution; these situations require an evaluation of each and a selection of the best alternative."*

The illustration shows a simple balance scale weighing "ALT #1" against "ALT #2" — visually representing step 3: literally weighing alternatives against each other.

### Qualitative Characteristics of Accounting Information

![[qualitative_characteristics.png]]

Split into two tiers:

**Fundamental Characteristics** (the "must-haves"):
- **Relevance**
- **Faithful Representation**

**Enhancing Characteristics** (the "nice-to-haves" that make good information even better):
- **Verifiability**
- **Timeliness**
- **Understandability**
- **Comparability**

*(The slide presents just the names in a labeled diagram — Fundamental characteristics are the two that information absolutely must have to be useful at all; Enhancing characteristics improve the usefulness of information that already has the two fundamental ones.)*

### Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)

![[gaap_diagram.png]]

The diagram shows the flow: **Various users need financial information** ← **Financial Statements** (Balance Sheet, Income Statement, Statement of Owner's Equity, Statement of Cash Flows, Note Disclosure) ← built on top of → **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**.

- *"The accounting profession has developed standards that are generally accepted and universally practiced."*
- **GAAP definition:** *"Standards that are generally accepted and universally practiced. These standards indicate how to report economic events."*

![[standard_setting_bodies.png]]

**Standard-setting bodies** (memorize the three named):
- **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**
- **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)**
- **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**

**International Note (boxed on slide):** *"Over 100 countries use International Financial Reporting Standards (called IFRS). For example, all companies in the European Union follow international standards. The differences between U.S. and international standards are not generally significant."*

### Accounting Principles (4 named principles)

![[accounting_principles.png]]

- **Historical Cost Principle** (or cost principle) — *"dictates that companies record assets at their cost."* (i.e., what you originally paid, not what it's "worth" now.)
- **Fair Value Principle** — *"states that assets and liabilities should be reported at fair value (the price received to sell an asset or settle a liability)."*
- **Accrual Principle** — *"states that Revenues and expenses are recorded when they are earned or incurred, regardless of when cash physically changes hands."* (This is a big one — it's *why* some of the Softbyte transactions later record revenue/expense even before or after cash actually moves.)
- **Conservatism Principle** — *"states that Anticipated losses and liabilities must be recognized as soon as they are discovered, whereas revenues and gains are only recorded when completely certain."* (In plain terms: when in doubt, lean toward *not* overstating how good things look — recognize bad news early, good news only once it's certain.)

### Assumptions (2 named)

![[assumptions.png]]

- **Monetary Unit Assumption** — *"requires that companies include in the accounting records only transaction data that can be expressed in terms of money."* (If it can't be given a dollar/taka value, it doesn't go in the accounting records — e.g., employee morale isn't "recordable" even though it matters to the business.)
- **Economic Entity Assumption** — *"requires that activities of the entity be kept separate and distinct from the activities of its owner and all other economic entities."* (The business's finances and the owner's *personal* finances must never be mixed together in the records — this is why, later, when Ray Neal invests personal cash into Softbyte, or later withdraws cash for personal use, both get carefully tracked as distinct transactions rather than ignored.)

**Forms of Business Ownership** (the entities this assumption applies to):
- Proprietorship
- Partnership
- Corporation

![[forms_of_business_ownership.png]]

| | Proprietorship | Partnership | Corporation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ownership | Owned by **one** person | Owned by **two or more** persons | Ownership divided into **shares of stock** |
| Management | Owner is often manager/operator | — | — |
| Liability | Owner receives any profits, suffers any losses, and is **personally liable for all debts** | Generally **unlimited personal liability** | **Limited liability** |
| Legal structure | — | Governed by a **partnership agreement** | Separate legal entity organized under state corporation law |

*(This table format didn't exist as a table on the slide — the original was three side-by-side bullet lists; it's reorganized here into a table purely so the three forms are easier to compare row-by-row, but every bullet point from the original slide is preserved.)*

## LO3 — The Basic Accounting Equation

![[basic_equation.png]]

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Equity}$$

**Why this equation matters** — three points, straight from the slide:
- *"Provides the underlying framework for recording and summarizing economic events."* — literally every single transaction a business makes gets checked against this equation.
- *"Assets are claimed by either creditors or owners."* — this is the intuition behind why the equation balances: every single thing the business owns (an Asset) was paid for by *someone's* claim on it — either people the business owes money to (creditors → Liabilities) or the owner's own stake (Owner's Equity).
- *"If a business is liquidated, claims of creditors must be paid before ownership claims."* — this is *why* Liabilities is written before Owner's Equity in the equation; creditors have first legal priority.

### Assets — defined

- *"Resources a business owns."*
- *"Provide future services or benefits."*
- Examples listed: **Cash, Supplies, Inventory, Equipment, Land, Building, Furniture, Investment, Intangible Assets (Trademark, License, Franchise), Accounts Receivables, Prepaid Expense, Accrued Revenues** etc.

### Liabilities — defined

- *"Claims against assets (debts and obligations)."*
- *"Creditors (party to whom money is owed)."*
- Examples listed: **Accounts Payable, Notes Payable, Bank Loan, Bond**, (Accrued Expense: Any Expense Payable, Salaries and Wages Payable, Tax Payable), **Unearned Revenue** etc.

### Owner's Equity — defined

- *"Ownership claim on total assets."*
- *"Referred to as residual equity."* (i.e., what's *left over* for the owner after subtracting everything owed to creditors — "residual" literally means "what remains.")
- *"Investment by owners and revenues (+)"* — these **increase** Owner's Equity.
- *"Drawings and expenses (–)."* — these **decrease** Owner's Equity.

### The Expanded Accounting Equation

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owner's Capital} - \text{Owner's Drawings} + \text{Revenues} - \text{Expenses}$$

This "unpacks" Owner's Equity into its four moving parts. Let's define each:

**Increases in Owner's Equity:**
- **Investments by owner** — *"the assets the owner puts into the business."*
- **Revenues** — *"result from business activities entered into for the purpose of earning income."* Common sources listed: **sales, fees, services, commissions, interest, dividends, royalties, and rent.**

**Decreases in Owner's Equity:**
- **Drawings** — *"An owner may withdraw cash or other assets for personal use."*
- **Expenses** — *"the cost of assets consumed or services used in the process of earning revenue."* Common expenses listed: **salaries expense, rent expense, utilities expense, tax expense**, etc.

**One-line memory device:** Capital and Revenue **grow** the business's equity; Drawings and Expenses **shrink** it. This is exactly the plus/minus pattern in the expanded equation above.

## LO4 — Transaction Analysis: The Math Section 🧮

### First, the filter: what even counts as a "transaction"?

- *"**Transactions** are a business's economic events recorded by accountants."*
- *"If an economic event **changes the financial situation** of the business (changes assets, liabilities, and owners' equity), it is a transaction."*
- *"Each transaction has a **dual effect** for its amount on the accounting equation (**Double Entry System**)."* — this is the single most important sentence in this whole section: **every transaction touches the equation in at least two places, by the exact same dollar amount, so the equation never goes out of balance.**

### The Record / Don't Record test

![[transaction_criterion.png]]

**Illustration 1-7** poses this exact question for three sample events: *"Is the financial position (assets, liabilities, or owner's equity) of the company changed?"*

| Event | Does it change assets/liabilities/equity? | Record? |
|---|---|---|
| Purchase computer | Yes — cash decreases, equipment increases | ✅ **Yes** |
| Discuss product design with potential customer | No — nothing has actually been exchanged yet, it's just a conversation | ❌ **No** |
| Pay rent | Yes — cash decreases, expenses increase (which decreases equity) | ✅ **Yes** |

**The exam-ready rule in one sentence:** *if money, property, or a legal obligation hasn't actually changed hands (or been earned/incurred) yet, it is not a transaction and does not get recorded — no matter how business-related the conversation might be.*

### 🧮 THE FULL WORKED EXAMPLE: Softbyte — all 10 transactions, with running balances

**The scenario:** Ray Neal starts a smartphone app development company called **Softbyte**. We are going to track every single transaction using the expanded accounting equation, and — this is the important part for your exam — **verify after every single transaction that the equation still balances.** The account columns we're tracking are:

$$\underbrace{\text{Cash} + \text{Accounts Receivable} + \text{Supplies} + \text{Equipment}}_{\text{Assets}} = \underbrace{\text{Accounts Payable}}_{\text{Liabilities}} + \underbrace{\text{Owner's Capital} - \text{Owner's Drawings} + \text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses}}_{\text{Owner's Equity}}$$

We'll start every account at **$0** and build up.

---

#### Transaction 1 — Investment by Owner

![[t1_investment.png]]

> *"On September 1, 2025, [Ray Neal] invests $15,000 cash in the business. This transaction results in an equal increase in assets and owner's equity."*

**The math:**
- Cash: $0 + 15{,}000 = \$15{,}000$
- Owner's Capital: $0 + 15{,}000 = \$15{,}000$

**Running balances after T1:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $15,000 |
| Accounts Receivable | $0 |
| Supplies | $0 |
| Equipment | $0 |
| **Total Assets** | **$15,000** |
| Accounts Payable | $0 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | **$15,000** |

✅ **Check:** $15{,}000 = 0 + 15{,}000$ — balances.

**Why this makes sense:** the owner's personal cash becomes the business's cash (Asset ↑), and simultaneously the owner now has a $15,000 *claim* on the business for having put that money in (Owner's Capital ↑). Same dollar amount, two sides — the "dual effect" in action.

---

#### Transaction 2 — Purchase of Equipment for Cash

![[t2_equipment.png]]

> *"Softbyte purchases computer equipment for $7,000 cash."*

**The math:**
- Cash: $15{,}000 - 7{,}000 = \$8{,}000$
- Equipment: $0 + 7{,}000 = \$7{,}000$

**Running balances after T2:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $8,000 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$15,000** |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | **$15,000** |

✅ **Check:** $15{,}000 = 0 + 15{,}000$ — still balances.

**Why this makes sense:** this is a straight **swap within Assets** — cash goes down by exactly the same amount equipment goes up. Total Assets doesn't change at all ($15,000 before, $15,000 after), and neither does equity. This is the simplest kind of transaction: one asset converts into another asset.

---

#### Transaction 3 — Purchase of Supplies on Credit

![[t3_supplies.png]]

> *"Softbyte purchases for $1,600 headsets and other accessories expected to last several months. The supplier allows Softbyte to pay this bill in October."*

**The math:**
- Supplies: $0 + 1{,}600 = \$1{,}600$
- Accounts Payable: $0 + 1{,}600 = \$1{,}600$

**Running balances after T3:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $8,000 |
| Supplies | $1,600 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$16,600** |
| Accounts Payable | $1,600 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | **$16,600** |

✅ **Check:** $16{,}600 = 1{,}600 + 15{,}000$ — balances.

**Why this makes sense:** notice **cash did not move at all** here — because it says "the supplier allows Softbyte to pay this bill in October," meaning this was bought **on credit** (a promise to pay later). So the asset (Supplies) went up, but instead of Cash going down to pay for it, a **Liability** (Accounts Payable — money the business now owes) went up instead. This is the key lesson of this transaction: **"on credit" or "on account" always means Accounts Payable increases, not Cash decreasing.**

---

#### Transaction 4 — Services Performed for Cash

![[t4_services_cash.png]]

> *"Softbyte receives $1,200 cash from customers for app development services it has performed."*

**The math:**
- Cash: $8{,}000 + 1{,}200 = \$9{,}200$
- Revenue: $0 + 1{,}200 = \$1{,}200$

**Running balances after T4:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $9,200 |
| Supplies | $1,600 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$17,800** |
| Accounts Payable | $1,600 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| Revenue | $1,200 |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | **$17,800** |

✅ **Check:** $17{,}800 = 1{,}600 + (15{,}000+1{,}200) = 1{,}600+16{,}200 = 17{,}800$ — balances.

**Why this makes sense:** the business *earned* money by doing actual work (app development services), so this is **Revenue**, not a capital investment — Revenue is one of the "+" items in the expanded equation. Both Cash (an Asset) and Revenue (part of Equity) go up together by the same $1,200.

---

#### Transaction 5 — Purchase of Advertising on Credit

![[t5_advertising_credit.png]]

> *"Softbyte receives a bill for $250 from the Daily News for advertising on its online website but postpones payment until a later date."*

**The math:**
- Accounts Payable: $1{,}600 + 250 = \$1{,}850$
- Expense: $0 + 250 = \$250$ (this **decreases** equity, so we'll track it separately then subtract)

**Running balances after T5:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $9,200 |
| Supplies | $1,600 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$17,800** (unchanged) |
| Accounts Payable | $1,850 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| Revenue | $1,200 |
| Expense | ($250) |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | $1{,}850 + 15{,}000 + 1{,}200 - 250 = \$17{,}800$ |

✅ **Check:** $17{,}800 = 17{,}800$ — balances.

**Why this makes sense — and why this one trips people up:** notice that **no cash moved, and no asset changed at all.** This is because of the **Accrual Principle** you learned back in LO2: *"expenses are recorded when they are incurred, regardless of when cash physically changes hands."* Softbyte received an advertising bill — the expense has already been **incurred** (the ad already ran), even though payment is postponed. So: Accounts Payable (Liability) increases by $250, and Expense (which decreases Equity) also "increases" by $250 — but since Expense is subtracted in the equation, the net effect on Equity is actually a **decrease**. That's why Total Assets stayed exactly the same ($17,800) while the *makeup* of the right-hand side shifted.

---

#### Transaction 6 — Services Performed for Cash and Credit

![[t6_services_cash_credit.png]]

> *"Softbyte performs $3,500 of services. The company receives cash of $1,500 from customers, and it bills the balance of $2,000 on account."*

**The math (this one has THREE effects at once — notice $1{,}500+2{,}000=3{,}500$):**
- Cash: $9{,}200 + 1{,}500 = \$10{,}700$
- Accounts Receivable: $0 + 2{,}000 = \$2{,}000$
- Revenue: $1{,}200 + 3{,}500 = \$4{,}700$

**Running balances after T6:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $10,700 |
| Accounts Receivable | $2,000 |
| Supplies | $1,600 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$21,300** |
| Accounts Payable | $1,850 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| Revenue | $4,700 |
| Expense | ($250) |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | $1{,}850+15{,}000+4{,}700-250=\$21{,}300$ |

✅ **Check:** $21{,}300 = 21{,}300$ — balances.

**Why this makes sense:** this transaction proves that a single business event can affect **more than 2 accounts at once** — but the *dual effect rule* still holds, because the *total* dollar increase on the Assets side ($1{,}500 + \$2{,}000 = \$3{,}500$) still exactly equals the total dollar increase on the Equity side (Revenue $+\$3{,}500$). **Accounts Receivable** is a new concept here: it means "customers owe *us* money" — the opposite of Accounts Payable. Since the $2,000 wasn't paid in cash yet, it becomes an asset (a right to collect cash later), not cash itself.

---

#### Transaction 7 — Payment of Expenses

![[t7_pay_expenses.png]]

> *"Softbyte pays the following expenses in cash for September: office rent $600, salaries and wages of employees $900, and utilities $200."*

**The math (three separate expenses, but all paid in cash together — total $1,700):**
- Cash: $10{,}700 - 1{,}700 = \$9{,}000$
- Expense (rent): $+600$
- Expense (salaries): $+900$
- Expense (utilities): $+200$
- Total new expense this transaction: $600+900+200=\$1{,}700$
- Running total Expense: $250+1{,}700 = \$1{,}950$

**Running balances after T7:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $9,000 |
| Accounts Receivable | $2,000 |
| Supplies | $1,600 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$19,600** |
| Accounts Payable | $1,850 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| Revenue | $4,700 |
| Expense | ($1,950) |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | $1{,}850+15{,}000+4{,}700-1{,}950=\$19{,}600$ |

✅ **Check:** $19{,}600 = 19{,}600$ — balances.

**Why this makes sense:** unlike Transaction 5 (where the expense was incurred but *not yet paid*, so it hit Accounts Payable), here the expenses are **paid immediately in cash**, so Cash (Asset) goes straight down, and Expense (which reduces Equity) goes straight up by the same total amount ($1,700). Notice $600+900+200$ really does equal $1{,}700$, and check it against the change in cash: $10{,}700-9{,}000=1{,}700$ ✓ — matches exactly.

---

#### Transaction 8 — Payment of Accounts Payable

![[t8_pay_payable.png]]

> *"Softbyte pays its $250 Daily News bill in cash. The company previously (in Transaction 5) recorded the bill as an increase in Accounts Payable."*

**The math:**
- Cash: $9{,}000 - 250 = \$8{,}750$
- Accounts Payable: $1{,}850 - 250 = \$1{,}600$

**Running balances after T8:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $8,750 |
| Accounts Receivable | $2,000 |
| Supplies | $1,600 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$19,350** |
| Accounts Payable | $1,600 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| Revenue | $4,700 |
| Expense | ($1,950) |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | $1{,}600+15{,}000+4{,}700-1{,}950=\$19{,}350$ |

✅ **Check:** $19{,}350 = 19{,}350$ — balances.

**Why this makes sense — the trap to avoid:** it is very tempting to think "we're paying a bill, so this must be an expense again." **It is NOT.** The expense was already recorded back in **Transaction 5**, the moment the bill was *incurred* (accrual principle again). Transaction 8 is purely a **settling of debt**: Cash (Asset) goes down, Accounts Payable (Liability) goes down, by the exact same amount. Equity does not change at all here — compare the Owner's Equity math to Transaction 7: it's untouched.

---

#### Transaction 9 — Receipt of Cash on Account

![[t9_receive_on_account.png]]

> *"Softbyte receives $600 in cash from customers who had been billed for services (in Transaction 6)."*

**The math:**
- Cash: $8{,}750 + 600 = \$9{,}350$
- Accounts Receivable: $2{,}000 - 600 = \$1{,}400$

**Running balances after T9:**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $9,350 |
| Accounts Receivable | $1,400 |
| Supplies | $1,600 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$19,350** (unchanged) |
| Accounts Payable | $1,600 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| Revenue | $4,700 |
| Expense | ($1,950) |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | $1{,}600+15{,}000+4{,}700-1{,}950=\$19{,}350$ |

✅ **Check:** $19{,}350 = 19{,}350$ — still balances, and notice Total Assets **didn't change at all** ($19,350 both before and after).

**Why this makes sense — this is basically the mirror image of Transaction 8:** just like Transaction 8 was a pure swap on the Liabilities side (Cash down, Payable down), Transaction 9 is a pure swap on the **Assets side** (Cash up, Accounts Receivable down, by the same $600). **The revenue was already recorded back in Transaction 6** when it was *earned* — collecting the cash later doesn't create new revenue, it just converts one asset (a "promise to pay" / Accounts Receivable) into another asset (actual Cash).

---

#### Transaction 10 — Withdrawal of Cash by Owner

![[t10_withdrawal_final.png]]

> *"Ray Neal withdraws $1,300 in cash from the business for his personal use."*

**The math:**
- Cash: $9{,}350 - 1{,}300 = \$8{,}050$
- Owner's Drawings: $0 + 1{,}300 = \$1{,}300$ (this **decreases** equity)

**Final running balances after T10 (the complete picture):**

| Account | Balance |
|---|---|
| Cash | $8,050 |
| Accounts Receivable | $1,400 |
| Supplies | $1,600 |
| Equipment | $7,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$18,050** |
| Accounts Payable | $1,600 |
| Owner's Capital | $15,000 |
| Owner's Drawings | ($1,300) |
| Revenue | $4,700 |
| Expense | ($1,950) |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | $1{,}600 + 15{,}000 - 1{,}300 + 4{,}700 - 1{,}950 = \$18{,}050$ |

✅ **FINAL CHECK:** $\$18{,}050 = \$18{,}050$ — **the equation balances perfectly**, exactly matching the slide's final numbers: *"$18,050 = $18,050"*.

**Why this makes sense — and why it's different from an expense:** withdrawing cash for **personal** use is **not a business expense** — the business didn't buy anything or consume any service to earn revenue. It's the owner taking *their own equity* back out of the business. This connects straight back to the **Economic Entity Assumption** from LO2: even though it's "his own money" in a sense, the business and the owner are treated as **separate entities** in the records, so this withdrawal must be tracked distinctly as **Drawings**, not folded into Capital or Expenses.

## LO7 (IFRS Appendix) — A Look at IFRS

### Similarities between GAAP and IFRS

- *"The basic techniques for recording business transactions are the same for U.S. and international companies."*
- *"Both international and U.S. accounting standards emphasize transparency in financial reporting. Both sets of standards are primarily driven by meeting the needs of investors and creditors."*
- *"The three most common forms of business organizations, proprietorships, partnerships, and corporations, are also found in countries that use international accounting standards."*

### Differences between GAAP and IFRS

- **Naming/authorship:** *"International standards are referred to as International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), developed by the International Accounting Standards Board. Accounting standards in the United States are referred to as generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) and are developed by the Financial Accounting Standards Board."*
- **Philosophy:** *"IFRS tends to be simpler in its accounting and disclosure requirements; some people say it is more 'principles-based.' GAAP is more detailed; some people say it is more 'rules-based.'"*
- **Regulation scope:** *"The internal control standards applicable to Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX) apply only to large public companies listed on U.S. exchanges. There is continuing debate as to whether non-U.S. companies should have to comply with this extra layer of regulation."*

### Looking to the Future

> *"Both the IASB and the FASB are hard at work developing standards that will lead to the elimination of major differences in the way certain transactions are accounted for and reported."*

## 📝 Quick Recall Sheet (all definitions in one place)

| Term | Definition (one line) |
|---|---|
| Accounting | Identifies, records, and communicates economic events to interested users |
| Bookkeeping | Just the recording part of accounting — not the whole process |
| Internal users | People inside the org (Marketing, Management, Finance, HR) |
| External users | People outside the org (Creditors, Investors, Auditors, Tax Authorities, Regulators, Customers, Labor Unions) |
| Financial Accounting | Serves both internal and external users |
| Managerial Accounting | Serves only internal users (management) |
| GAAP | Generally accepted, universally practiced reporting standards |
| Historical Cost Principle | Record assets at their original cost |
| Fair Value Principle | Report at price to sell asset/settle liability |
| Accrual Principle | Record revenue/expense when earned/incurred, not when cash moves |
| Conservatism Principle | Recognize losses early, gains only when certain |
| Monetary Unit Assumption | Only record what can be expressed in money |
| Economic Entity Assumption | Keep business records separate from owner's personal activities |
| Assets | Resources the business owns |
| Liabilities | Debts/obligations owed to creditors |
| Owner's Equity | Owner's residual claim on assets |
| Basic equation | Assets = Liabilities + Owner's Equity |
| Expanded equation | Assets = Liabilities + Capital − Drawings + Revenue − Expenses |
| Transaction | An economic event that changes assets, liabilities, or equity — has a dual effect |
| Double-entry system | Every transaction affects the equation in at least two places, by equal dollar amounts |
