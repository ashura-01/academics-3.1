## 1. Demand & Supply (4 marks)

### 1.1 The Law of Demand

The **law of demand** states that, holding everything else constant (*ceteris paribus*), there is an **inverse relationship** between the price of a good and the quantity demanded of it: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, and as price falls, quantity demanded rises.

This relationship is shown visually as the **demand curve**, which slopes downward from left to right.

### 1.2 Change in Quantity Demanded vs. Change in Demand

This is the single most commonly confused pair of terms in introductory economics, and it is exactly what the syllabus is testing.

| | **Change in Quantity Demanded** | **Change in Demand** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | A change in the **price of the good itself** | A change in any **non-price determinant** (income, tastes, price of related goods, expectations, number of buyers) |
| Effect on graph | **Movement along** the same, fixed demand curve | The **entire curve shifts** — right (increase) or left (decrease) |
| Terminology | "Quantity demanded rises/falls" | "Demand increases/decreases" |
| Example | Coffee price drops from $4 to $3 → you buy more cups (movement from point A to point B on curve *D*) | Coffee is scientifically shown to improve health → *at every price*, people now want more coffee (curve *D1* shifts right to *D2*) |

![[attachments/01_movement_vs_shift_demand.png]]

**Non-price determinants that shift the demand curve** (cause a *change in demand*):
- **Income** — for a *normal good*, higher income shifts demand right; for an *inferior good*, higher income shifts demand left.
- **Price of related goods** — a rise in the price of a *substitute* (e.g., Pepsi) shifts demand for the good (e.g., Coke) right; a rise in the price of a *complement* (e.g., burger buns) shifts demand for the paired good (e.g., burger patties) left.
- **Tastes and preferences** — a trend or fad increases demand; going out of fashion decreases it.
- **Expectations** — expecting prices to rise next month increases demand today.
- **Number of buyers** — a larger market (e.g., population growth) shifts demand right.

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> If the question changes the **price of the good on the x/y-axis itself**, the answer is *"movement along the curve / change in quantity demanded."* If the question changes **anything else** (income, weather, a related good's price, advertising, expectations), the answer is *"shift of the curve / change in demand."*

### 1.3 Why Is the Demand Curve Downward Sloping?

Two well-known economic effects together explain why people buy more of a good when its price falls:

#### 1. Substitution Effect

- **Mechanism:** Relative Price Shift
- **Definition:** When the price of Good A falls while Good B remains constant, Good A becomes relatively cheaper. Consumers rationally **substitute** away from the relatively expensive Good B toward the cheaper Good A.
- **Core Rule:** Always moves in the opposite direction of price (Price $\downarrow$ Quantity Demanded $\uparrow$).
- **Exam Example:** If the price of **Chicken** drops from $\$10$ to $\$5$ while **Beef** remains at $\$10$, consumers switch their buying preference to Chicken because it offers a better relative bargain.

#### 2. Income Effect

- **Mechanism:** Real Purchasing Power Shift
- **Definition:** When a price drops, the consumer's nominal income (actual money) remains unchanged, but their **real income** (purchasing power) increases. The consumer feels richer because their fixed budget can stretch further.
- **Core Rule:** Impact depends on whether the item is a _normal_ or _inferior_ good.
- **Exam Example:** If your weekly **Rice** expense drops from $\$50$ to $\$30$, your nominal budget has an extra $\$20$ left over. You feel richer, so you buy more Rice (or upgrade to other goods) using that saved purchasing power.

Together, Substitution Effect + Income Effect ⇒ **quantity demanded rises as price falls**, which is exactly the downward slope of the demand curve.

![[attachments/02_downward_sloping_demand.png]]

**A third, more technical justification** (useful if you want to go deeper): the **law of diminishing marginal utility**. 

- **Less Extra Satisfaction:** Every extra unit of a good gives the consumer less additional satisfaction (marginal utility) than the previous unit.
    
- **Rational Decision:** consumer will only buy another unit if its price drops enough to match that lower level of satisfaction.
    
- **Why Prices Must Fall:** Lower prices are required to convince consumers to buy larger quantities.

### Example

- **1st Bottle of Water:** You are extremely thirsty, so the extra satisfaction is **high** $\rightarrow$ You are willing to pay **full price**.
    
- **2nd Bottle:** Your thirst is quenched, so the extra satisfaction is **lower** $\rightarrow$ You are only willing to buy it at a **reduced price**.
    
- **3rd Bottle:** You are no longer thirsty, so the extra satisfaction is **almost zero** $\rightarrow$ You will only take it if it is **extremely cheap or on sale**.

> [!tip] Exam Tip — likely 4-mark answer structure
> 1. Define the law of demand (inverse price–quantity relationship).
> 2. Explain **substitution effect** with a one-line example.
> 3. Explain **income effect** with a one-line example.
> 4. (Bonus) Tie it to diminishing marginal utility.
> 5. Draw a simple downward-sloping *D* curve with P on the y-axis and Q on the x-axis.

### 1.4 Supply — quick contrast (for completeness)
The **law of supply** is the mirror image: price and quantity *supplied* are **directly (positively) related** — as price rises, producers are willing to supply more, because production becomes more profitable at higher prices. The supply curve slopes **upward**. The same movement-vs-shift logic applies: a change in the good's own price causes a *movement along* the supply curve (change in quantity supplied); a change in input costs, technology, number of sellers, taxes/subsidies, or expectations causes the *entire supply curve to shift* (change in supply).

## 2. Utility Theory (6 marks)

### 2.1 What Is Utility?

**Utility** is a measure of the satisfaction, happiness, or benefit a person gets from consuming a good or service.

**Cardinal Utility Approach**: assumes utility is measurable in quantitative units called **utils**. Example: your first bottle of Mojo gives you 10 utils, the second gives 9 utils, the third gives 8 utils, and so on.

**Ordinal Utility Approach**: assumes utility cannot be measured numerically — it can only be **ranked**. Example: "I simply prefer Xiaomi over Samsung" is realistic; "I get exactly 50 utils from Xiaomi and 40 from Samsung" is very hard to actually prove. This is the more modern, realistic approach and is the basis of indifference curve analysis below.

**Total Utility (TU)** — the total satisfaction a person receives from consuming a given quantity of a good.

**Marginal Utility (MU)** — the *additional* utility from consuming one more unit of a good:

$$MU = \frac{\Delta TU}{\Delta Q}$$

**Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility**: as consumption of a good increases, the marginal utility from each additional unit tends to **decline**. The first unit gives the most satisfaction, the second slightly less, and so on. Notice that TU can still be *rising* even while MU is *falling* — they can move in opposite directions.

![[attachments/03_total_marginal_utility.png]]

**Worked example (Water–Diamond Paradox):** water is essential to life (very high *total* utility) but cheap, while diamonds are non-essential but expensive. Why? Water is abundant, so we consume a lot of it — by the time we drink our daily glasses, the *marginal* value of one more glass is very low, so we're only willing to pay a low price. Diamonds are scarce, so we consume very little — the marginal utility of one more diamond stays very high, so we're willing to pay a high price. **Price tracks marginal utility, not total utility.**

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> If asked to explain the water–diamond paradox, the key phrase examiners look for is: *"price is determined by marginal utility, not total utility."*

### 2.2 The Budget Constraint

The **budget constraint (budget line)** shows all the combinations (bundles) of two goods a consumer can afford, given a fixed money income and the prices of the two goods.

It is built from **three components**: the price of good X, the price of good Y, and the consumer's income.

**General equation:**
$$I = P_X Q_X + P_Y Q_Y$$

#### Worked Example
Moni has a monthly income of **I = $1,200**. Burgers (X) cost **$100**, Cold drinks (Y) cost **$80**.

$$1200 = 100 Q_X + 80 Q_Y$$

- If she spends *all* income on X: $Q_X = 1200/100 = 12$ units.
- If she spends *all* income on Y: $Q_Y = 1200/80 = 15$ units.

Plotting these two intercepts (12 on the X-axis, 15 on the Y-axis) and connecting them with a straight line gives the budget constraint. Any point **on** the line, or **inside** the triangle formed with the origin, is an affordable bundle.

![[attachments/04_budget_constraint_basic.png]]

**Slope of the budget constraint** = the relative price of the two goods:
$$\text{Slope} = \frac{P_X}{P_Y} = \frac{100}{80} = 1.25$$
This means 1 unit of X costs the same as 1.25 units of Y (X's opportunity cost, in terms of Y, is 1.25).

#### 2.2.1 What Changes the Budget Constraint

**(a) A change in the price of X (income and $P_Y$ constant):**
- Price of X **falls** (e.g., $100 → $60): max affordable X rises (12 → 20). The line **rotates outward** on the X-axis (pivoting from the fixed Y-intercept). More bundles become available.
- Price of X **rises** (e.g., $100 → $150): max affordable X falls (12 → 8). The line **rotates inward toward the origin**. Fewer bundles are available.
- The same logic applies symmetrically to a change in $P_Y$, rotating the line along the Y-axis instead.
- **Key rule:** a change in *either* price changes the **slope** of the budget line (relative prices change), while the *unchanged*-axis intercept stays fixed.

![[attachments/05_budget_constraint_changes.jpg]]

**(b) A change in income (both prices constant):**
- Income **rises** (e.g., $1,200 → $1,600): both intercepts move outward (X: 12→16, Y: 15→20). The line shifts **rightward, parallel** to the original.
- Income **falls** (e.g., $1,200 → $800): both intercepts move inward (X: 12→8, Y: 15→10). The line shifts **leftward, parallel** to the original.
- **Key rule:** a change in income shifts the line **parallel** (slope unchanged, since relative prices haven't changed) — only its distance from the origin changes.

> [!tip] Exam Tip — Rule of thumb for "graph the change" questions
> - **Price change → line rotates** (pivots on one fixed intercept, slope changes).
> - **Income change → line shifts parallel** (both intercepts move by the same proportion, slope unchanged).

#### 2.2.2 Practice Problems (from the course handout — solve these by hand)
Diagram each budget constraint and calculate the slope at each stage:

1. (i) Income = 4000; $P_X$ = 50; $P_Y$ = 100. (ii) Income falls to 3000, prices unchanged. (iii) After the income drop, $P_X$ falls to 40, $P_Y$ unchanged.
2. (i) Income = 3000; $P_X$ = 25; $P_Y$ = 200. (ii) Only $P_X$ increases to 30. (iii) Income stays 3000, $P_X$ stays 25, but $P_Y$ drops to 160.
3. (i) Income = 2000; $P_X$ = 40; $P_Y$ = 150. (ii) Income doubles, prices unchanged. (iii) After the income boost, $P_Y$ rises to 250, $P_X$ unchanged.

**Method for every part:** find the two intercepts ($Q_X^{max} = I/P_X$ at $Q_Y=0$; $Q_Y^{max} = I/P_Y$ at $Q_X=0$), plot them, connect with a straight line, then compute slope $= P_X/P_Y$.

### 2.3 Preferences and Indifference Curves

An **indifference curve** shows all the bundles of two goods that give the consumer the **same** level of satisfaction (total utility) — the consumer is *indifferent* between any two points on the same curve.

![[attachments/06_indifference_map.jpg]]

**Slope of an indifference curve = Marginal Rate of Substitution (MRS)** — the rate at which a consumer is willing to trade one good for the other while staying equally satisfied:
$$MRS = \frac{MU_X}{MU_Y}$$

![[attachments/07_two_indifference_curves_mrs.jpg]]

#### The Four Properties of Indifference Curves

**Property 1 — Higher curves are preferred.** Since more consumption generally means more satisfaction, a curve farther from the origin ($I_5 > I_4 > I_3...$) represents higher utility.

**Property 2 — Downward sloping.** If a consumer likes both goods, giving up some of one good must be compensated with more of the other to keep satisfaction constant — hence the negative slope.

**Property 3 — Indifference curves never cross.** If two curves crossed, transitive logic would force a contradiction: points A and B (same curve 1) would be equally satisfying, B and C (same curve 2) would be equally satisfying, so A and C should be equally satisfying too — but C has strictly more of both goods, so it must be *preferred* to A. This contradiction proves curves can't intersect.

![[attachments/08_indifference_curves_cannot_cross.jpg]]

**Property 4 — Bowed inward (convex to the origin).** People are more willing to give up a good they have in *abundance* and less willing to give up a good they have *little* of, so the MRS diminishes as you move down the curve (the curve gets flatter). Example: with lots of Mojo and little pizza, you'll happily trade 6 Mojo for 1 pizza (MRS = 6); once you have lots of pizza and little Mojo, you'll only trade 1 Mojo for 1 pizza (MRS = 1).

![[attachments/09_convex_indifference_curve_mrs.jpg]]

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> If asked to *"state and explain the properties of indifference curves,"* write **all four properties** with the one-line reasoning behind each — this is a very common exam question and each property is usually worth separate marks.

### 2.4 The Consumer's Optimum

The consumer picks the point on the budget constraint that lies on the **highest attainable indifference curve**. This point is called the **optimum**, where the budget line is **tangent** to (just touches) an indifference curve.

At the optimum, the slope of the indifference curve (MRS) equals the slope of the budget line (relative price):
$$\frac{P_X}{P_Y} = \frac{MU_X}{MU_Y}$$

![[attachments/10_consumer_optimum.jpg]]

In the diagram: the consumer *would* prefer point A, but it lies above (outside) the budget line, so it's unaffordable. Point B is affordable but lies on a lower indifference curve, so it's sub-optimal. The **tangency point** is the best affordable bundle.

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> "Why not point A?" → *unaffordable, lies outside the budget constraint.* "Why not point B?" → *affordable but sub-optimal, lies on a lower indifference curve than what's achievable.*

### 2.5 Two Extreme Cases of Indifference Curves

- **Perfect Substitutes** → straight-line indifference curves, constant MRS. The consumer trades one good for the other at a fixed rate no matter how much of each they have (e.g., two identical brands of bottled water, or BSRM Steel vs. KSRM Steel of identical quality).
- **Perfect Complements** → L-shaped (right-angle) indifference curves. The goods only add value when consumed together in a fixed ratio (e.g., left shoes and right shoes — 5 left shoes + 1 right shoe gives the same satisfaction as 1 pair).

![[attachments/11_perfect_substitutes_complements.jpg]]

## 3. Elasticity (4 marks — no math required, focus on curves)

### 3.1 Why We Need Elasticity

The **law of demand** only tells us the *direction* of the price–quantity relationship (inverse), not *how much* quantity responds to a price change. **Price elasticity of demand (Ed)** measures the *degree* of responsiveness — the percentage change in quantity demanded for a given percentage change in price:

$$E_d = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P}$$

*(You won't be tested on the midpoint-formula calculation for this section, but understanding that elasticity = "percentage change in quantity" ÷ "percentage change in price" is essential to reading the curves correctly.)*

By convention, economists report the **absolute value** of Ed (drop the minus sign), since price and quantity demanded always move in opposite directions.

### 3.2 The Spectrum of Demand Elasticity

| Type | Condition | Curve shape | Real-world intuition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elastic** | $E_d > 1$ | Relatively **flat** | %ΔQ > %ΔP — buyers are very responsive (e.g., branded snacks with many substitutes) |
| **Inelastic** | $E_d < 1$ | Relatively **steep** | %ΔQ < %ΔP — buyers are not very responsive (e.g., necessities like salt) |
| **Unit Elastic** | $E_d = 1$ | Rectangular hyperbola | %ΔQ = %ΔP exactly |
| **Perfectly Elastic** | $E_d = \infty$ | **Horizontal** line | Any tiny price rise sends quantity demanded to zero — buyers switch entirely to identical alternatives |
| **Perfectly Inelastic** | $E_d = 0$ | **Vertical** line | Quantity demanded doesn't change at all, regardless of price — e.g., a life-saving drug like insulin |

![[attachments/12_elasticity_of_demand_spectrum.png]]

> [!tip] Exam Tip — reading the diagrams
> - **Flatter curve = more elastic** (more responsive to price).
> - **Steeper curve = more inelastic** (less responsive to price).
> - The two extremes are the easiest marks: **horizontal = perfectly elastic**, **vertical = perfectly inelastic**. Don't mix these up — students very commonly swap them.

### 3.3 Elasticity of Supply

**Price elasticity of supply (Es)** measures the responsiveness of quantity *supplied* to a change in price:
$$E_s = \frac{\%\Delta Q_s}{\%\Delta P}$$

Same classification as demand: **elastic** ($E_s>1$), **inelastic** ($E_s<1$), **unit elastic** ($E_s=1$), **perfectly elastic** ($E_s=\infty$, horizontal supply curve), **perfectly inelastic** ($E_s=0$, vertical supply curve).

![[attachments/13_elasticity_of_supply_spectrum.jpg]]

**Real-world examples:**
- **Perfectly elastic supply**: local labor markets tied to a strict minimum wage — if a factory offers even slightly below the minimum wage, labor supply drops to zero.
- **Perfectly inelastic supply**: land in a specific, fixed location — no matter how high the price goes, no more of that exact land can be produced.

### 3.4 Other Elasticity Concepts (concepts only, for context)

- **Cross elasticity of demand ($E_c$)** — responsiveness of quantity demanded of one good to a price change in *another* good.
  - $E_c > 0$ → the goods are **substitutes** (e.g., raising the price of Pepsi increases demand for Coke).
  - $E_c < 0$ → the goods are **complements** (e.g., raising the price of burger patties decreases demand for burger buns).
- **Income elasticity of demand ($E_y$)** — responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in income.
  - $E_y > 0$ → **normal good** (demand rises as income rises).
  - $E_y < 0$ → **inferior good** (demand falls as income rises).

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> Since this topic is marked "no math" on your syllabus, prioritize being able to: (1) draw and label all five demand-curve elasticity cases, (2) draw and label all five supply-curve elasticity cases, (3) explain *why* each extreme case looks the way it does with a real-world example, and (4) state the sign rule for substitutes/complements and normal/inferior goods.

## Quick Revision Summary

- **Demand & Supply**: price change → movement along curve (Δ quantity demanded); non-price factor → curve shifts (Δ demand). Downward slope ⟵ substitution effect + income effect (+ diminishing MU).
- **Utility Theory**: Cardinal (measurable utils) vs. Ordinal (rankable) utility. Budget line: $I = P_XQ_X + P_YQ_Y$, slope $=P_X/P_Y$; price change rotates it, income change shifts it parallel. Indifference curves: 4 properties (higher-preferred, downward-sloping, non-crossing, convex); optimum where $MRS = P_X/P_Y$.
- **Elasticity**: $E_d = \%\Delta Q_d / \%\Delta P$. Flatter = more elastic, steeper = more inelastic. Horizontal = perfectly elastic, vertical = perfectly inelastic. Same logic mirrored for supply.
