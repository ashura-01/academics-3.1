## 1. Demand & Supply (4 marks)

### 1.1 The Law of Demand

The **law of demand** states that, holding everything else constant (*ceteris paribus*), price and quantity demanded are **inversely related**: as price rises, quantity demanded falls; as price falls, quantity demanded rises. This is shown as a **downward-sloping demand curve**.

### 1.2 Change in Quantity Demanded vs. Change in Demand

This distinction is the most commonly tested (and most commonly confused) idea in this topic.

| | **Change in Quantity Demanded** | **Change in Demand** |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | A change in the **price of the good itself** | A change in a **non-price determinant** (income, tastes, price of related goods, expectations, number of buyers) |
| Effect on graph | **Movement along** the same, fixed demand curve | The **entire curve shifts** — right (increase) or left (decrease) |
| Terminology | "Quantity demanded rises/falls" | "Demand increases/decreases" |
| Example | Coffee price drops $4 → $3 → you buy more cups (movement A → B along curve *D*) | Coffee is shown to improve health → *at every price*, people want more coffee (curve *D1* shifts right to *D2*) |

![[attachments/01_movement_vs_shift_demand.png]]

**Non-price determinants that shift the demand curve** (cause a *change in demand*):
- **Income** — normal good: higher income shifts demand right. Inferior good: higher income shifts demand left.
- **Price of related goods** — substitute's price ↑ shifts this good's demand right (e.g., Pepsi price ↑ → more demand for Coke). Complement's price ↑ shifts this good's demand left (e.g., burger bun price ↑ → less demand for burger patties).
- **Tastes and preferences** — a trend increases demand; falling out of fashion decreases it.
- **Expectations** — expecting prices to rise next month increases demand today.
- **Number of buyers** — a larger market shifts demand right.

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> Price of the good itself changes → **"movement along the curve / change in quantity demanded."** Anything else changes → **"shift of the curve / change in demand."**

### 1.3 Why Is the Demand Curve Downward Sloping?

Two effects together explain why people buy more of a good when its price falls:

**1. Substitution Effect**
- When the price of Good A falls while Good B stays the same, A becomes **relatively cheaper**. Consumers rationally substitute *away* from B and *toward* A.
- **Example:** if Chicken drops from $10 to $5 while Beef stays at $10, consumers switch toward Chicken because it's now the better relative bargain.

**2. Income Effect**
- When a price drops, nominal (money) income is unchanged, but **real income (purchasing power)** effectively rises — the same budget now buys more.
- **Example:** if your weekly Rice bill drops from $50 to $30, you have an extra $20 of purchasing power, so you buy more Rice (or other goods) with it.

Substitution Effect + Income Effect ⇒ **quantity demanded rises as price falls** ⇒ downward-sloping demand curve.

![[attachments/02_downward_sloping_demand.png]]

**Third justification (bonus depth): Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility.** Each extra unit of a good gives less additional satisfaction than the one before it, so a rational consumer will only buy another unit if the price falls enough to match that lower satisfaction — which is exactly why lower prices are needed to sell larger quantities.
- *1st bottle of water:* very thirsty → high extra satisfaction → willing to pay full price.
- *2nd bottle:* thirst mostly quenched → lower extra satisfaction → only buy at a reduced price.
- *3rd bottle:* not thirsty at all → satisfaction near zero → only buy if extremely cheap.

> [!tip] Exam Tip — likely 4-mark answer structure
> 1. Define the law of demand (inverse price–quantity relationship).
> 2. Explain **substitution effect** with a one-line example.
> 3. Explain **income effect** with a one-line example.
> 4. (Bonus marks) Tie it to diminishing marginal utility.
> 5. Draw a simple downward-sloping *D* curve, P on the y-axis, Q on the x-axis.

### 1.4 Supply — same logic, mirrored

The **law of supply**: price and quantity supplied are **directly (positively) related** — as price rises, producers supply more because production becomes more profitable. The supply curve slopes **upward**.

- **Movement along the supply curve** (change in *quantity supplied*) — caused by a change in the good's **own price**. Example: laptop price drops → sellers slide down the curve and produce fewer units.
- **Shift of the entire supply curve** (change in *supply*) — caused by non-price factors: input costs, technology, number of sellers, taxes/subsidies, expectations. Example: microchip costs fall → laptops are cheaper to make → the whole supply curve shifts right.

## 2. Utility Theory (6 marks) — Budget Constraint

### 2.1 Utility, TU, and MU — what the words mean

- **Utility** = the satisfaction, happiness, or benefit a person gets from consuming a good.
- **TU (Total Utility)** = the *total* satisfaction from consuming a given quantity of a good. Example: eating 3 slices of pizza gives you some total amount of happiness — that's TU at Q=3.
- **MU (Marginal Utility)** = the *extra* (additional) satisfaction from consuming **one more** unit, on top of what you already had. Formula:
$$MU = \frac{\Delta TU}{\Delta Q}$$
where $\Delta TU$ = "change in Total Utility" and $\Delta Q$ = "change in Quantity" (i.e., +1 unit). Example: if TU from 2 slices of pizza = 19 utils, and TU from 3 slices = 27 utils, then $MU$ of the 3rd slice $= 27-19 = 8$ utils.
- In practice, MU usually **falls** as you consume more of something (you're less hungry after each slice) — this is the *Law of Diminishing Marginal Utility*, and it's part of *why* demand curves slope downward (see §1.3 above).

Now that TU/MU are defined, here's the actual graded content: the **budget constraint**.

### 2.2 What the Budget Constraint Is

Given a fixed income, a consumer can't buy unlimited quantities of everything. The **budget constraint (budget line)** shows every combination (bundle) of **two goods** — call them Good X and Good Y — that a person **can afford**.

**What each symbol means:**
- $I$ = **Income** — the total money the consumer has to spend.
- $P_X$ = **Price of Good X** (per unit, e.g., in dollars). $Q_X$ = **Quantity of Good X** — how many units of X they buy.
- $P_Y$ = **Price of Good Y** (per unit). $Q_Y$ = **Quantity of Good Y** — how many units of Y they buy.
- "Good X" is plotted on the **horizontal axis**, "Good Y" on the **vertical axis** — this is just a labeling convention (X = horizontal, like x-axis; Y = vertical, like y-axis).

**General equation** (money spent on X + money spent on Y = total income):
$$I = P_X Q_X + P_Y Q_Y$$

### 2.3 Worked Example

Moni has a monthly income of **I = $1,200**. Burgers (X) cost **$100**, Cold drinks (Y) cost **$80**.

$$1200 = 100 Q_X + 80 Q_Y$$

- Spend it all on X: $Q_X = 1200/100 = 12$ units.
- Spend it all on Y: $Q_Y = 1200/80 = 15$ units.

Plot these two intercepts (12 on the X-axis, 15 on the Y-axis) and join them with a straight line — that's the budget constraint. Any point **on** the line, or **inside** the triangle formed with the origin, is affordable.

![[attachments/03_budget_constraint_basic.png]]

**Slope of the budget constraint** = relative price of the two goods:
$$\text{Slope} = \frac{P_X}{P_Y} = \frac{100}{80} = 1.25$$
This means 1 unit of X costs the same as 1.25 units of Y.

### 2.4 What Changes the Budget Constraint

**(a) A change in the price of X (income and $P_Y$ constant):**
- $P_X$ **falls** (e.g., $100 → $60): max affordable X rises (12 → 20). The line **rotates outward** on the X-axis, pivoting on the fixed Y-intercept. More bundles become available.
- $P_X$ **rises** (e.g., $100 → $150): max affordable X falls (12 → 8). The line **rotates inward** toward the origin. Fewer bundles are available.
- The same logic applies to $P_Y$, rotating the line along the Y-axis instead.
- **Key rule:** a change in *either* price changes the **slope** (relative prices change); the axis intercept for the *unchanged* good stays fixed.

![[attachments/04_budget_constraint_changes.jpg]]

**(b) A change in income (both prices constant):**
- Income **rises** ($1,200 → $1,600): both intercepts move outward (X: 12→16, Y: 15→20). The line shifts **rightward, parallel** to the original.
- Income **falls** ($1,200 → $800): both intercepts move inward (X: 12→8, Y: 15→10). The line shifts **leftward, parallel** to the original.
- **Key rule:** income change shifts the line **parallel** — slope is unchanged since relative prices haven't changed, only distance from the origin changes.

> [!tip] Exam Tip — Rule of thumb for "graph the change" questions
> - **Price change → line rotates** (pivots on one fixed intercept, slope changes).
> - **Income change → line shifts parallel** (both intercepts scale together, slope unchanged).

### 2.5 Practice Problems — Fully Solved

**Method for every part (memorize this):**
$$Q_X^{max} = \frac{I}{P_X}\ (\text{at } Q_Y=0) \qquad Q_Y^{max} = \frac{I}{P_Y}\ (\text{at } Q_X=0) \qquad \text{Slope} = \frac{P_X}{P_Y}$$
Plot the two intercepts, join with a straight line.

---

#### Problem 1
*(i) Income = 4000; $P_X$=50; $P_Y$=100. (ii) Income falls to 3000, prices unchanged. (iii) After the income drop, $P_X$ falls to 40, $P_Y$ unchanged.*

**(i)** $I=4000,\ P_X=50,\ P_Y=100$
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{4000}{50}=80 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{4000}{100}=40 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{50}{100}=0.5$$

**(ii)** Income falls to 3000 (prices unchanged)
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{3000}{50}=60 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{3000}{100}=30 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{50}{100}=0.5$$
*Slope unchanged (relative prices didn't move) → parallel shift inward.*

**(iii)** $I=3000$, $P_X$ falls to 40 ($P_Y=100$ unchanged)
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{3000}{40}=75 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{3000}{100}=30 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{40}{100}=0.4$$
*Y-intercept stays at 30; X-intercept rotates outward 60→75 (X got cheaper). Slope falls 0.5→0.4.*

| (ii) Income ↓ to 3000 | 60 | 30 | 0.5 | Parallel shift inward |
| (iii) $P_X$ ↓ to 40 | 75 | 30 | 0.4 | Rotates outward on X-axis |

#### Problem 2
*(i) Income = 3000; $P_X$=25; $P_Y$=200. (ii) Only $P_X$ increases to 30. (iii) Income stays 3000, $P_X$ stays 25, but $P_Y$ drops to 160.*

**(i)** $I=3000,\ P_X=25,\ P_Y=200$
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{3000}{25}=120 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{3000}{200}=15 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{25}{200}=0.125$$

**(ii)** $P_X$ rises to 30 ($I=3000$, $P_Y=200$ unchanged)
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{3000}{30}=100 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{3000}{200}=15 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{30}{200}=0.15$$
*Y-intercept stays at 15; X-intercept shrinks 120→100 (X got pricier) → rotates inward on X-axis. Slope rises 0.125→0.15.*

**(iii)** Back to $P_X=25$, but $P_Y$ drops to 160 ($I=3000$ throughout)
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{3000}{25}=120 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{3000}{160}=18.75 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{25}{160}=0.15625$$
*Vs. stage (i): X-intercept back to 120, Y-intercept rises 15→18.75 (Y got cheaper) → rotates outward on Y-axis. Slope rises 0.125→0.156.*

#### Problem 3
*(i) Income = 2000; $P_X$=40; $P_Y$=150. (ii) Income doubles, prices unchanged. (iii) After the income boost, $P_Y$ rises to 250, $P_X$ unchanged.*

**(i)** $I=2000,\ P_X=40,\ P_Y=150$
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{2000}{40}=50 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{2000}{150}=13.33 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{40}{150}=0.267$$

**(ii)** Income doubles to 4000 (prices unchanged)
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{4000}{40}=100 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{4000}{150}=26.67 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{40}{150}=0.267$$
*Both intercepts exactly double, slope unchanged at 0.267 → textbook parallel outward shift from higher income.*

**(iii)** $I=4000$, $P_Y$ rises to 250 ($P_X=40$ unchanged)
$$Q_X^{max}=\frac{4000}{40}=100 \qquad Q_Y^{max}=\frac{4000}{250}=16 \qquad \text{Slope}=\frac{40}{250}=0.16$$
*X-intercept stays at 100; Y-intercept falls 26.67→16 (Y got pricier) → rotates inward on Y-axis. Slope falls 0.267→0.16.*

![[attachments/07_practice_problem_3.png]]

|---|---|---|---|---|
| (i) Original | 50 | 13.33 | 0.267 | — |

> [!tip] Exam Tip — the one pattern behind all three problems
> - **Only income changes** → both intercepts scale by the same factor, slope stays exactly the same, line moves **parallel**.
> - **Only one price changes** → the intercept on *that good's own axis* moves, the *other* intercept stays fixed, slope changes, line **rotates** around the fixed intercept.

## 3. Elasticity (4 marks — no math, focus on curves)

### 3.1 Why We Need Elasticity

The law of demand only tells us the *direction* of the price–quantity relationship, not *how much* quantity responds to a price change. **Price elasticity of demand ($E_d$)** measures that responsiveness — the percentage change in quantity demanded for a given percentage change in price:

$$E_d = \frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P}$$

*(No calculation required for this topic — but knowing that elasticity = "% change in quantity" ÷ "% change in price" is what lets you read the curves correctly.)* By convention, economists use the **absolute value** of $E_d$ (drop the minus sign), since price and quantity demanded always move in opposite directions.

### 3.2 The Spectrum of Demand Elasticity

| Type | Condition | Curve shape | Real-world intuition |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Elastic** | $E_d > 1$ | Relatively **flat** | %ΔQ > %ΔP — buyers very responsive (e.g., branded snacks with many substitutes) |
| **Inelastic** | $E_d < 1$ | Relatively **steep** | %ΔQ < %ΔP — buyers not very responsive (e.g., necessities like salt) |
| **Unit Elastic** | $E_d = 1$ | Rectangular hyperbola | %ΔQ = %ΔP exactly |
| **Perfectly Elastic** | $E_d = \infty$ | **Horizontal** line | Any tiny price rise sends quantity demanded to zero — buyers switch entirely to identical alternatives |
| **Perfectly Inelastic** | $E_d = 0$ | **Vertical** line | Quantity demanded doesn't change at all regardless of price — e.g., a life-saving drug like insulin |

![[attachments/08_elasticity_of_demand_spectrum.png]]

> [!tip] Exam Tip — reading the diagrams
> - **Flatter curve = more elastic** (more responsive to price).
> - **Steeper curve = more inelastic** (less responsive to price).
> - The two extremes are the easiest marks: **horizontal = perfectly elastic**, **vertical = perfectly inelastic**. Don't swap these — it's the most common mistake.

### 3.3 Elasticity of Supply

**Price elasticity of supply ($E_s$)** measures the responsiveness of quantity *supplied* to a price change:
$$E_s = \frac{\%\Delta Q_s}{\%\Delta P}$$

Same five-way classification as demand: **elastic** ($E_s>1$), **inelastic** ($E_s<1$), **unit elastic** ($E_s=1$), **perfectly elastic** ($E_s=\infty$, horizontal supply curve), **perfectly inelastic** ($E_s=0$, vertical supply curve).

![[attachments/09_elasticity_of_supply_spectrum.jpg]]

**Real-world examples:**
- **Perfectly elastic supply:** local labor markets tied to a strict minimum wage — if a factory offers even slightly below minimum wage, labor supply drops to zero.
- **Perfectly inelastic supply:** land in one fixed location — no matter how high the price goes, no more of that exact land can be produced.

> [!tip] Exam Tip
> Since this topic is "no math," prioritize being able to: (1) draw and label all five demand-curve elasticity cases, (2) draw and label all five supply-curve elasticity cases, (3) explain *why* each extreme case looks the way it does with a real-world example.

## Quick Revision Summary

- **Demand & Supply:** price change → movement along curve (Δ quantity demanded); non-price factor → curve shifts (Δ demand). Downward slope ⟵ substitution effect + income effect (+ diminishing MU as bonus depth).
- **Utility Theory:** Budget line $I = P_XQ_X + P_YQ_Y$, slope $=P_X/P_Y$. Price change → line **rotates** (one intercept fixed). Income change → line **shifts parallel** (both intercepts scale together). All 3 practice problems solved above — the pattern always repeats.
- **Elasticity:** $E_d = \%\Delta Q_d/\%\Delta P$. Flatter = more elastic, steeper = more inelastic. Horizontal = perfectly elastic, vertical = perfectly inelastic. Same logic mirrored for supply.
